Jakub: I was talking with PDs and asking about this, and a) we should have about 200k in our budget, and b) we should be spending all that money this year.

David: That's right. The goal is to spend down the budget this year. Then we'll get a new budget to spend down next year.

Jakub: That's important context for what comes next. Given that we have more money than we thought, I'd like us to think about allocating this money to specific things, one being the Funding team. As a status update, we have 4 initial fundees. We'll announce them next week, hopefully (depending on how the paperwork goes). It's the team lead of rustup as a full-time MiR, someone from clippy as a part-time MiR, and two other grants on the libs team and the clippy team, and then we'll continue with another batch after that. But the budget is coming from RF anyway, and they want the funding program to succeed. So I don't think it's necessarily in a state that the Council has to give the funds. Maybe the RF can match it from other sources. There's also the OpenAI membership fee. We're also taking about putting out an AlphaOmega grant for cargo maintainers or a specific team. We hope to start with a big bang and have many maintainers, as many as we can have within reason, for publicity, to show companies this is successful. I agree that this is a possible concern. We're discussing keeping some sort of buffer in reserve. This is yet-to-be-determined.

Support for adding more budget to Funding to demonstrate the value of doing this, particularly the MiR program. E.g., we can show that "we revitalized this many teams".

Jakub: We could alleviate this concern by preparing what our budget would be for the next year assuming no funding, and talking to the Foundation with that data, to see if they can match it. If not, we could allocate a bit less. It doesn't have to happen immediately (since the Foundation figures out budgeting at EOY).

Josh: To be explicit, that was not a concern, I think we should do it unconditionally from the Foundation's ability to match it. Just raising it for consideration. We should do it regardless.

### "Create an LLM committee" leadership-council#308

- **Link:** https://github.com/rust-lang/leadership-council/issues/308
- **Labels:** T-leadership-council, proposed-final-comment-period, disposition-merge, S-needs-decision, I-council-nominated

oli: There's some overlap with the above. The main thing is the intent to unblock progress, not the specific approach. What's the process for figuring this out? We can get the committee started, or some other process. I don't want to keep getting stuck. If we can talk about the committee and figure out if it's doable, and then we could discuss TC's proposal.

Josh: +1. Adding that I do think we should establish some useful baseline for the LLM policy as soon as we can. Unblock the mods team, but to explain what the team is doing and provide them with something to work from. We should establish that, but at the same time we shouldn't think of the LLM policy as something that's done once. One of the items that came up during the forge policy discussion was the feeling that no matter how much was written that it's possible to change the policy and adapt, there were concerns raised about feasibility. This led to the LLM committee being brought up as a possible solution. It's not sufficient to establish a policy. We need a process by which the policy is set, evolved and adapted to new circumstances. For that, there should be a group to do that.

TC: Contra to Josh, I don't know that we should be putting ourselves in a position of trying to change this policy all the time. I feel a policy of this nature should have some persistence, and we should be giving people a sense of comfort and stability. And so we should do the work to find something that solves the problems that are in front of us, and put that out there. The council previously put a policy into FCP.

I remain hopeful that by focusing on the problem that we have, which is things coming in from the outside that are putting stresses on reviewers, on moderators, and on the Project, that we can agree on a policy that will help to solve that.

Something that came up in discussion, from BurntSushi, is that by applying _a_ policy (even a fairly permissive one) to his repository, it significantly reduced the amount of influx he was receiving, because, apparently, these agents read that. We're doing ourselves damage by not just agreeing on a policy that represents the intersection of what we can all agree on.

And we've made progress in that. E.g., Jakub and I discussed between us and came up with something that both he and I could largely accept. If people who are coming at this from such different angles as Jakub and I are can find agreement, that points in a hopeful direction that compromise is possible. That remains my hope for this.

Manish: I agree with Josh and TC that having a baseline policy we all agree on now, fast, is good. It's not hard, in a baseline policy, to say that parts of the policy are likely to change. To express that it's temporary. It's nice to have stability; it's not great when policies change all the time. But you can at least give people an idea of where they will change. The world of LLMs changes fast enough. People are learning how to use these better. People are figuring out their boundaries over time. It takes time. Teams figuring out where they're comfortable is going to take time. And it's nice to have a team that can take in all that information — all of that experiential information. I don't think the LC is prepared to take in that firehose.

Josh: Very much in agreement with Manish. LLMs will disappoint anyone looking for comfort and stability. If we're going to provide people with some firm foundation to build on, we need a committee that will consider the landscape and its evolution appropriately. The idea is to provide a policy which is stable in ethos, not necessarily stable in text. The actual letter of the law could adapt to deal with "wait, LLMs can do that? Should they?"

oli: I don't expect us to be a metronome where rules flip back and forth. They should evolve organically. I want a specific group to do that and I think we can totally have a baseline policy (with basic rules we all agree on) and at the same time create a committee. We can set some clear rules that we all agree on and say that's the baseline, then say "go and continue with that". I don't see how else to do it.

David: One of the things that may have been missed in the discussion is that the point of the committee is that it can give people the ability to change it. The committee doesn't imply the rate of change, just implies that there could be change.

oli: What can we do to move this forward? We have both this issue and TC's open. Could we combine these somehow? What if I cancel FCP, then add a bunch of rules that would form the baseline when the committee is established and add the people I think should be there. Would people be fine with that? Could we then move ahead with the committee?

TC: I have serious reservations about delegating FCP authority. If we're looking for a compromise, for me, it would be creating a committee that produces proposals for the council. This is how other projects have done it. Recently, the GCC project adopted an AI policy. They did that by establishing a commitee that developed a policy and proposed it to their steering commitee, which then adopted it. That seems a workable model for us too.

I have reservations about the removal of autonomy from teams by creating a committee that has the power to create policies that override those of teams. In my view, the Project works at its best by having teams experiment. Then, the Council is the body that the teams feed into: they express their desires, concerns, and constraints through their Council representatives. Then we act on those things when creating Project-wide policies and taking other actions. I have reservations about indirecting that representation to another body.

From a practical POV, I see this committee as likely to have the same difficulties that we have. And I don't see, mechanically, how this will be easy to resolve. The proposal contains statements about how easy it will be for this committee to change the policy. But why should we expect it to be easy? The proposal requires the committee to agree unanimously. And it suggests that if the committee is struggling with that, it'll be raised to us to start removing its members. But if we have to step in, we'll be back in the same position. We'll have to ask, "well, what are member X's concerns? Are they reasonable?" Etc. We're not going to be able to drop context on this. So I don't think this proposal solves the problem that it sets out to solve. And meanwhile, it risks team autonomy and indirecting our accountability.

oli: This doesn't change anything if the committee can only make proposals because then the committee can't make a proposal to us because they can't agree on a proposal. I added rules for us, to make sure we have control, to use as fallback, but I don't expect them to trigger. If they do trigger often, then the idea just doesn't work.

Josh: I think that the net effect of having a team do this work and then bringing us the proposal... does anybody expect that we are likely to take that proposal and ratify it rather than effectively relitigating it and treating it as a baseline and doing all the same work? The point of delegating is to actually delegate. To help undeadlock this, suppose that the committee is free to ratify policies with the understanding that the only way the policy can apply to all teams without exception and not allow teams to override is that ultimately that would be a matter for moderation. If they propose a policy that teams can't override, then they need a sign-off from moderation, since otherwise it's not a Project-wide policy.

Manish: I'd like to point out that devolution can be contextual. I do share the concern that I don't want teams to lose the ability to decide to do something on this. Having the overarching moderation policy is good, but that's definitely something we should be very careful to tweak if it overrides teams completely. This team could come up with pieces of policy that teams could choose to use, or they can choose to delegate to them. The team could produce artifacts that include the research they've done on what people want and what they don't want. They can produce bits of policy, e.g., "here's a policy that applies to LLM PR descriptions", and then teams could say "yes, we like that policy". And maybe that goes in the baseline policy that becomes a default unless overridden by teams. I think a lot of Rust teams will just use the common policies, and some will use most of it and sign-off on importing changes. The power does not have to devolve to the LLM subcommittee; it's still with the teams, and the teams can move it back to the subcommittee. This can be a flexible system.

TC: I agree with Manish. For all the reasons that Manish said, I don't see the need to give this committee the power to override teams without council assent.

And, I wanted to specifically answer Josh's question of whether any of us believes that a committee could come up with a policy that we could then ratify. I believe that we could. If the commitee did what a subcommitee should do, which is to solicit the values of all of the stakeholders, including the council, and did the work to assemble something that fell within the intersection of what people could agree to, that we would then be very likely to simply adopt the recommendations of the committee.

oli: If the committee succeeds in making the policy that we can ratify, then the committee can ratify it themselves. We're not controlling the Funding Team's choices of who gets the MiR positions. The point of the Council is to delegate the work, and we can also delegate the decision process to not be blocked on us, while controlling the Committee.

Josh: Had a recent second-hand experience of what it means to completely delegate something. If you're going to successfully delegate, you'll find yourself thinking it's not how you would've done it but that's okay. If you don't think that, then you failed at delegation. I don't think this council is capable of doing that. We are extremely good at having opinions. We are good at getting into the details. I don't think we're capable, as a Council, to sign off and not relitigate. If we do not, in advance, say that we are committing to actually delegating this and that we will take back that authority or adjust that authority if something goes wrong, that is very different from saying we'll delegating drafting and try to sign off on the result. I think that will have very different outcomes.

Manish: As a small observation, for the funding decisions, I imagine that they're much less controversial than this. It's easy to delegate something where your need is "someone I trust has evaluated this", and that's it. For a funding decision, I'm happy to delegate that to someone I trust to evaluate it, but for this, it's hard to delegate views in that way. I do think that by delegating the writing, it's still possible to get good results by having them individually work with different stakeholders. If they know that they'll need council approval, they'll work with council members to explain what they're thinking and solicit objections. And the goal isn't necessarily for them to change too much what they're proposing, but to come to a shared understanding, just as by having a one-on-one conversation, TC and Jakub came to an understanding. People disagree on the axioms here, and that's probably not changing. What you really want is not something that's exactly what everyone wants, but you want everyone to be happy with what's being proposed. A delegated team that doesn't actually have power to make decisions is able to do that by working with everyone here and making sure they're happy with it individually. But maybe that doesn't work, and maybe they can't find something like that. Then the trick you're playing is shuffling things around to create a different group that's maybe able to come to consensus when another group isn't able to.

Remy: In my view, the point is to create a committee that will be able to come to consensus. At least I hope we can. And, two, I trust that we can find good people that can make the decisions. To me, it's easy to say "let's delegate to the committee" because I trust that they'll be good people who can work together to get to a decision and that the decision will be good. It's going to be hard to find something we all agree on and that's worthwhile. Even for the basic policy, we disagree on many things. We already see that for any item, there are already some teams that do that thing, and it's suggested we can't override them. I trust the people in the Rust Project, and I trust that we can form the right committee.

TC: What I trust are the teams. We have existing teams, throughout the Project, that make their own decisions about a wide range of policy matters. So when we're talking about removing autonomy from those teams — and saying, "well, that's OK because we trust some committee" — then I ask, "well, don't we trust the teams?" Because that's what I trust.

So that's why I have serious reservations about us seeming to impose on these teams by creating a committee that has the power to override them and, by pushing the hard decisions to a committee, diluting our direct accountability for that.

Mara: Isn't it exactly the people on those teams asking us to do this? You're saying we're removing authority from the teams, but the teams are asking us to do something about it.

Manish: Well, there are dev-tools teams that I've been paying attention to that aren't really talking about this that much. Maybe some teams are having problems and others are not. I want the dev-tools team to start talking about this because it's going to be something that people will care about. This supports TC's point that it's not all the teams, and the teams are different here and are behaving differently.

Josh: 1) If we choose to enact a process with a given outcome (deadlock, not creating it), we're responsible for resulting inaction. If we try and that results in a lack of policy, we're ultimately responsible for the inaction. 2) If the point of contention continues to be whether the committee can enact policies that teams cannot override, then I'll reiterate that "suppose the committee is capable of doing the policies, and the teams are subject to team override unless moderation team signs off on them". Would that be enough?

TC: I don't think it's the role of the moderation team. I think that's a council role. And so I would not consider that to be a compromise position.

Manish: I think that can fit in the role of the moderation team in a sense. If you're assuming that the moderation team only applies policy that comes to a moderation level. There are two ways of reading this. One is that the moderation is an escape hatch and could sign off on policies broadly on LLMs. And I don't think that's the actual thing that we want to happen. The moderation team currently can apply Project-wide policies that reach a certain bar of disruption. I don't have a definition for the bar of disruption — I know it when I see it.

Josh: The policy vacuum is handled in the same way. We ban people for bot-driven contributions over the entire rust-lang org, ignoring team boundaries.

oli: With my mod hat, we wouldn't be doing random decisions that aren't already something we will need to enforce. Moderation enforces moderation things. We're not there to enforce our own biases, because if we did, there wouldn't be any LLM contributions and various people would have been banned. That's not happening. I like Josh's proposal; it's a good balance. Mods aren't going to go on power trips, as we've seen in other organizations. That's not the point of it.

Jakub: TC and Oli, let's say the committee had permission to establish Project-wide policy that can be overridden by teams. And to establish policy that can't be overridden, that would have to be approved either by the council or both by the council and the mods. Would that be OK by TC and Oli?

TC: That's closer. I'd need to see the details, and it would come down to those. It's not a good topic for the end of the meeting.
